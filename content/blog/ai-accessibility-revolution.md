# AI Is an Accessibility Revolution. The Productivity People Just Found It First.

The whole AI conversation is based on the wrong argument.

Everyone measures AI in productivity. Tokens per second. Emails answered. Meetings summarized. Hours trimmed from the workweek.

Wrong question.

I'll get to the right one. First I have to tell you about my eyes.

Before writing got hard, reading did.

Books upon books upon books. That is what built me. I was made out of borrowed paperbacks, late nights, strange little obsessions, the books I could explain and the books I could not. Reading was not a hobby. It was intake. Compost. Spellwork. The private machinery that turns a person into a writer.

Then college ended, my eyes got worse, and the act got harder every year, until the thing that made me a writer became a thing I had to ration.

You do not notice the door closing. You notice you have stopped walking through it.

In my thirties, I stopped being able to read trade paperbacks.

Not all at once. That would have been cleaner, in a terrible way. It happened in the humiliating incremental style disability loves. First I held the book under better light. Then farther away. Then closer. Then I took breaks. Then I made bargains. One more chapter. One more page. One more paragraph before the headache won.

At first, I bounced hard.

Not elegantly. Not bravely. Hard.

Fuck this, I thought. It is not meant for me anymore.

Then I tried ebooks. I did the thing disabled people always do: invent a workaround, then a workaround for the workaround, then pretend the stack of compromises is the same as access. Reading became a special occasion. Text blown up to 50 points, my thumb scrolling because a single paragraph no longer fit on the screen.

Technically, I could read.

Technically, a person can eat soup with a fork.

What came back was only part of the thing. The words were larger, yes, but the world was smaller. The flow was gone. The casualness was gone. The ability to pick up a book because it looked interesting and disappear into it for three hours was gone. Reading had become an event I had to prepare for, budget for, recover from.

That is not the same thing.

And even that assumed the book I wanted existed in a format I could use at all.

I had not just lost literature. I had lost the compost heap where my imagination lived.

I made my peace with it. You lose things, you adjust, you call it maturity so you do not have to call it grief. Reading was going to be a thing I used to do.

AI changed the way I work.

That turned out to be the least interesting thing about it.

The deepest part is that AI gave me literature back.

What came back was not just function. It was private wanting. The ability to reach for a book, a draft, a strange little obsession, without first turning myself into a request.

Now I can feed manuscripts into ElevenLabs and get serviceable output that lets me continue to enjoy the craft I love more than anything. Not perfect output. Not magic. Serviceable. And serviceable is enough to reopen a door.

And it is not one product, or one trick. Transcription. OCR. Summarization. Text-to-speech. Voice input. Image description.

The whole messy harness, pointed at a life instead of a workflow.

Accessibility does not always arrive as a miracle. Sometimes it arrives as good enough. Good enough to hear the rhythm of a paragraph. Good enough to catch where a sentence limps. Good enough to feel a scene breathe. Good enough to stay in relationship with the work.

I can hear structure again.

I can hear pacing. Dialogue. Repetition. The wobble in a paragraph that looked fine on the screen but falls apart in the ear. I can listen to manuscripts when my eyes will not cooperate. I can remain inside the conversation of fiction, not as a visitor, not as someone waiting outside the locked door, but as a writer among writers.

That is not productivity.

That is restoration.

It is the same with menus. I do not have to nudge friends to read them for me anymore. I can snap a picture of a menu and ask an AI harness to analyze it with my own context and help me figure out what I want to eat that day.

For a sighted person, that might sound like an indulgence.

For me, it is dignity with a side of fries.

Because needing help is not shameful, but having no choice is exhausting. There is a specific fatigue that comes from turning every small act into a negotiation. Can you read this? Can you help me with that? What does that sign say? What are my options? What am I missing? What am I allowed to want before I become inconvenient?

I have spent my life knowing I would need to rely on the kindness of strangers.

AI did not remove the need for people. That is not the point. The point is that, for the first time in my life, I have a choice about when I need that kindness.

Choice is the revolution.

Privacy is the revolution.

Unapproved wanting is the revolution.

This is the part sighted people often miss: disabled access is frequently mediated access.

Someone else owns the gate. Someone else buys the software, approves the device, curates the catalog, writes the policy, decides which need is legitimate and which desire is suspicious. That means disabled people do not only lose access when something is physically inaccessible. We lose access when someone else decides our wants are too strange, too adult, too frivolous, too embarrassing, or too much trouble to accommodate.

It is hard when your reading life runs through someone else's approval. An agency. A catalog. A funding rule. A committee deciding what counts as legitimate access.

They might approve the respectable stuff. The useful stuff. The professionally legible stuff. They are not going to approve, say, bizarro fiction about a person involuntarily transformed into a stuffed animal.

But that matters.

The weird stuff matters. The profane stuff matters. The queer stuff matters. The embarrassing stuff matters. The little what-the-hell-did-I-just-read books matter. Writers are not built only out of classics and approved materials. We are built out of obsessions, accidents, small presses, genre trash, private fixations, and art that would make a grant committee sweat through its blazer.

And the gatekeeping is not limited to books.

Think about what it means that the design of a screen reader can even raise the question of whether it should speak profanity aloud. Not whether a blind person should have access to the text as written. Whether the machine should protect them from it. The blind user becomes an object to be managed instead of a reader with taste, judgment, privacy, and a right to the whole sentence.

That same logic shows up everywhere. Once access is something granted by decent people for decent uses, the forbidden rooms appear fast.

Disabled people's sexual needs are ignored because the subject makes nondisabled people uncomfortable. Our desire becomes a problem to route around. Our curiosity becomes a risk category. We are treated as if access should return us only to the clean, useful, innocent parts of life — the medical portal, never the filthy sentence.

But people are not built out of approved materials.

The opposite of access is not always exclusion. Sometimes it is permission. Permission to read this, but not that. Permission to work, but not wander. Permission to need, but not want.

Disabled people are disproportionately vulnerable to moral panic because so much of our access already depends on permission. If your reading life depends on approved formats, approved catalogs, approved tools, approved funding, approved use cases, then every panic has a handle. Someone can always say: not that book, not that word, not that body, not that desire.

This is why the productivity conversation feels so small to me.

The people measuring AI by how many emails it can summarize are not wrong, exactly. They are counting the handrail and missing the door.

Here is the reframe: AI is not first a productivity tool. It is an accessibility tool that productivity people happened to find first.

The people who understand its real value are not the early adopters trimming an hour off their inbox. They are the people who have spent their whole lives negotiating agency with a world that was not built for them. We know the value of access because we know what it costs to ask for the key.

And the stairs are everywhere.

The stairs are tiny UI text. The stairs are dim restaurants and chalkboard menus. The stairs are tools that assume vision, focus, memory, motor control, and stamina are abundant. The stairs are the blank page when your eyes are already spent. The stairs are every system that says access is available, provided your need is respectable enough to survive committee review.

When you have lost something, you do not measure a tool by how much faster it makes you.

You measure it by what it gives back.

The three hours came back. Not my eyes. The disappearing. The ordinary miracle of losing an afternoon to a book without first negotiating with my own body.

AI gives some people speed. Fine. Lovely. Put it on a slide with a tasteful blue gradient.

But speed is not the miracle.

The miracle is wanting something privately and reaching for it without filing a case for your own legitimacy. A book. A menu. A draft. A filthy sentence. A strange little paperback no committee would ever approve. A story you were afraid your body had taken from you.

The programmer who lost their vision. The novelist with tremors. The artist with chronic fatigue who can finish a piece before the fatigue wins. The kid with dyslexia who has a whole story in them and finally has a way to get it out. The blind adult who wants the whole sentence, profanity included. The disabled person who wants access not only to work and bills and medical portals, but to pleasure, weirdness, art, sex, trash, beauty, and the private territory of their own taste.

AI did not make productive people more productive.

That is the boring part.

The story is that it handed agency back to people the industry had spent years locking out, then called the ramp a workflow improvement because nondisabled people noticed they could run up it.

That is the feature.

So ask the other question.

Not what AI does for the already productive.

Ask who it gives back to.

Ask what doors it reopens.

Ask who gets to read again, write again, choose again, want again.

Then tell me this is about productivity.
